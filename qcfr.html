Grammaire

être + adjective — the core pattern

The most fundamental sentence structure in French. Subject + être + adjective. Nothing goes between them.

Elle est belle.  /  Tu es seul.

Grammatical gender — a first look

Every French noun is either masculine (m) or feminine (f). This is not about biology — it's a grammatical category that affects the words around the noun (articles, adjectives). There is no reliable rule to predict gender, so the best habit is to always learn a noun with its article: le café, la fille.

The articles tell you the gender at a glance:

MasculineFeminine
singularle / unla / une
pluralles / des (both)
before vowell' (both)

In this lesson: le café (m), la fille (f). More nouns and their genders will build up lesson by lesson.

Adjective agreement

Adjectives in French match the gender (and number) of the noun they describe. Masculine is the base form; feminine usually adds -e, and the pronunciation often changes too.

seul → seule  ·  beau → belle  ·  amoureux → amoureuse

Notice: beau → belle is irregular (not just +e). These exceptions are worth memorising one by one.

Stressed pronouns — moi / toi

When a pronoun stands alone, comes after a preposition, or is used for emphasis at the start of a sentence, French uses a special stressed form. Think of it like saying "as for me…" or "you, specifically."

Moi, je suis…  /  Toi, tu es…

You'll also see it after comme: comme toi (like you), comme moi (like me).

Subject pronouns and the conjugation table

French verbs change their ending depending on who is doing the action. The conjugation table you see for être lists all six possibilities. Each row pairs a subject pronoun with its corresponding verb form.

You will encounter this table format for every new verb. The pattern of six forms — je, tu, il/elle, nous, vous, ils/elles — is the same every time, so it is worth memorising now.

tu vs vous — two ways to say "you"

French has two words for "you" and choosing the right one matters socially.

tu is used with one person you know well — a friend, a family member, a child, or a peer. In this lesson, Jacques and Marcel use tu because they are close friends.

vous is used in two situations: with a stranger or someone older/senior (as a sign of respect), or when speaking to more than one person at once. In Lesson 2, when Jacques meets Mireille for the first time, he uses vous.

A good default rule for a new learner: when in doubt, use vous. Switching to tu too early can feel presumptuous; being offered tu by the other person is generally a good sign you can reciprocate.

Korean speaker note

In Korean you can often drop the subject entirely ("예뻐", "혼자야") and the sentence is perfectly natural. In French, the subject is almost always stated explicitly: Elle est belle, Il est seul. Getting used to always saying the pronoun is one of the first habits to build.

This sound does not exist in Korean or English. It is produced at the back of the throat, not the front of the mouth. Imagine the feeling just before you clear your throat — a soft, continuous friction, never a tap or a trill.

ɑ̃ Nasal vowels — les voyelles nasales

The tilde (˜) over a vowel in IPA means the sound is nasalized — air flows through both your mouth and your nose at the same time. The consonant that follows (n, m) is not pronounced separately — it just nasalizes the vowel before it.

In English, vowels before n or m get slightly nasal (think: can't, sand), but French takes this much further — the nasality is full and deliberate. Korean has no nasal vowels at all, so this takes conscious practice.

💡 devant [dəvɑ̃] — the -ant ending is nasal. The n is swallowed into the vowel. Say "dev-ah" but let the sound resonate through your nose at the end. Same for vraiment [vʁɛmɑ̃].
⚠️ Common mistake: keep your mouth open throughout the vowel. Do not close it to make a final [n] sound — that turns it into a regular vowel + n, which is wrong. The nasality should come entirely from letting air flow through the nose while the mouth stays open.

In French, when a word ending in a normally-silent consonant is followed by a word beginning with a vowel, the consonant "links" to the next word and is pronounced. This is called liaison.

Grammaire

-er verbs — the most common pattern

A large majority of French verbs end in -er in the infinitive. They all follow the same pattern: remove the -er, then add the endings below. This lesson introduces three: aimer, manger, habiter.

PronounEndingaimerhabiter
j'-ej'aimej'habite
tu-estu aimestu habites
il / elle-eil aimeil habite
nous-onsnous aimonsnous habitons
vous-ezvous aimezvous habitez
ils / elles-entils aimentils habitent

Important: the endings -e, -es, -ent are all silent — je, tu, and ils/elles forms sound identical. Only nous (-ons) and vous (-ez) are clearly distinct.

je → j' before a vowel

When a verb starts with a vowel or silent h, je contracts to j'. This is called elision.

je aime → j'aime  ·  je habite → j'habite

The h in habiter is silent and treated as a vowel for this purpose. This is why it's j'habite and not je habite.

Informal note: in casual spoken French, tu can also elide to t' before a vowel — for example t'aimes ça? instead of tu aimes ça? You will hear this a lot in everyday Quebec French, though it is not used in writing.

Yes/no questions with intonation

The simplest way to ask a yes/no question in spoken French is to say a normal statement but raise your voice at the end. The word order does not change at all.

Vous aimez le café?

This is very common in everyday speech. More formal question structures (est-ce que…, inversion) exist but are not needed yet.

tu vs vous — in practice

You saw this distinction introduced in Lesson 1. Here you see it applied. Jacques uses vous with Mireille because they have just met. This is the polite, respectful default with strangers.

Notice that vous changes the verb form: vous aimez, vous habitez. When you switch to tu with someone, both people usually switch at the same time — it signals a shift in the relationship.

Korean speaker note

The -er verb endings je/tu/il/ils (-e/-es/-e/-ent) all sound the same when spoken aloud. This means that in speech, context and pronouns carry most of the grammatical information — not the verb ending itself. This is unlike Korean, where verb endings change noticeably. The written forms differ, but spoken French relies heavily on the pronoun to know who is doing what.

Prononciation — Leçon 2
ɔ̃ The nasal vowel in bonjour

You already encountered nasal vowels in Lesson 1 (ɑ̃). The sound ɔ̃ is a second nasal vowel — made like ɔ (an open "oh" sound) but with air passing through the nose at the same time.

The n after the vowel is not pronounced separately. It is absorbed into the vowel to nasalize it.

💡 In English, the vowel in "song" or "long" is close but not nasal. In French, you add deliberate nasal resonance. Try humming a little through your nose as you say the vowel.
⚠️ Common mistake: keep your mouth open for the full duration of the vowel. A very frequent error is to close the mouth and add a final [n] or [ŋ] — bon-n instead of bɔ̃. The [n] must stay completely silent; only the nasal airflow through the nose should be heard.

When a word ending in a vowel (like je) meets a word starting with a vowel or silent h, the first vowel is dropped and replaced with an apostrophe. This keeps the speech flowing smoothly.

It is not optional — saying je aime instead of j'aime sounds unnatural and is grammatically wrong in standard French.

You saw liaison in Lesson 1 with vous êtes. Here it appears again with vous aimez and vous habitez. The silent s of vous links to the vowel that follows, sounding like [z].

Liaison is one of the main reasons French sounds so continuous and "blurred" to new learners. Words do not have as clear boundaries as in Korean or English.

h The silent h — habiter, hier…

In French, the letter h is almost always silent. It does not produce any sound. There are two types: h muet (silent h, triggers elision and liaison) and h aspiré (aspirate h, blocks elision and liaison) — but for now, treat all h as silent.

habiter starts with a silent h muet, which is why we say j'habite (not je habite) and nous habitons with liaison.

Grammaire

ton / ta — your first possessive adjective

Like all adjectives in French, possessives agree with the noun they accompany, not with the owner. ton goes before masculine nouns, ta before feminine nouns.

Gender of nounFormExample
masculinetonton café, ton numéro
femininetata cuisine, ta fille
before a vowel/hton (even for f.) — ton invitation

Note the last row: ta invitation would create a vowel clash, so French uses ton before any noun starting with a vowel or silent h, regardless of gender. This is the same elision principle you saw with j'.

ton numéro  ·  ta cuisine  ·  ton invitation (not ta)

aimer vs adorer — degrees of enthusiasm

French has a useful scale for expressing how much you like something. adorer is clearly stronger than aimer and is used freely in everyday speech — it doesn't carry the weight of "I'm in love with" the way it might in English.

ExpressionRough equivalent
j'aime bienI quite like / I'm fond of
j'aimeI like / I love
j'adoreI really love / I'm crazy about
J'aime les deux, mais j'adore la cuisine italienne.

Numbers and phone numbers

In French, phone numbers are typically read digit by digit. A few pronunciation notes worth memorising now:

six [sis] alone or at end of group, but the final x can be silent before a consonant. huit [ɥit] — the h is aspirate here, so no liaison with the preceding word. dix [dis] alone, but again the final consonant changes in connected speech.

For now, reading each digit clearly and separately is perfectly correct and how you'll give your number to someone.

4-6-3-5-7-1-2 → quatre six trois cinq sept un deux

quel est…? — asking "what is"

Quel is an interrogative adjective meaning "what" or "which". It agrees in gender and number with the noun that follows. For now, the most useful form is quel est + masculine noun, used to ask for someone's name, number, age, etc.

Quel est ton numéro?  ·  Quel est ton nom?

Korean speaker note

The possessive adjective system in French can feel unfamiliar because it agrees with the noun, not the owner — unlike Korean 내 (my) or 네 (your), which don't change based on what follows. In French, whether you're talking about a man's or a woman's possession doesn't affect the form; what matters is the gender of the thing possessed. This takes a little time to feel natural.

ø The close rounded vowel — deux,eux

You first saw this sound in Lesson 1 in amoureux [amuʁø]. It appears here in les deux [le dø]. This is the close (tighter mouth) version of the rounded front vowels.

To produce it: position your tongue as if to say the Korean vowel ㅔ or the French [e], then round your lips as if saying ㅗ. The result is ø. It is distinct from the open œ in seule — ø has a slightly more closed, tighter feel.

You already know ɑ̃ (Leçon 1) and ɔ̃ (Leçon 2). French has a third nasal vowel: ɛ̃, which appears in cinq, invitation, and bien. It is produced like the vowel ɛ (as in belle) but with nasal airflow through the nose at the same time.

y The close rounded front vowel — tu, sûr, huit

The vowel [y] does not exist in Korean or English. It appears in some of the most frequent words in French: tu [ty], sûr [syʁ], plus [ply], and — relevant to this lesson — huit [ɥit].

To produce [y]: position your tongue as if to say the Korean vowel ㅣ (or English "see"), then round your lips as if saying ㅜ, without letting your tongue move. The tension between the front tongue position and the rounded lips produces [y].

In huit, the [y] appears as a glide — written [ɥ] in IPA — because it precedes another vowel. The glide ɥ is simply [y] moving quickly into the next sound. It is pronounced in a single brief motion rather than held.

💡 The easiest way to practise: say ㅣ slowly, hold the sound, then gradually round your lips without stopping. You will pass through [y]. At first it will feel strange — the mouth position feels contradictory. That is normal and means you are doing it right.
⚠️ Do not substitute [u] (ㅜ) for [y]. tu [ty] ≠ tout [tu]. French speakers hear these as completely different sounds.

Telling the time

The basic frame is Il est + [hour] + heure(s). From there, minutes are added with specific expressions:

ExpressionMeaningExample

et demie:30 (half past)7h30 → sept heures et demie
moins quart:45 (quarter to)7h45 → huit heures moins quart
pileexactly on the hour7h00 → sept heures pile

Note that moins quart references the next hour: 7h45 = "eight o'clock minus a quarter."

Il est sept heures et demie.

24-hour vs 12-hour time

French uses both systems freely. Official contexts (transport, appointments, broadcast) typically use 24h: 19h30. Everyday conversation often uses 12h: sept heures et demie du soir. Both are correct and you will encounter both.

In this dialogue, Mireille switches to 7h/8h (12h format) while Jacques has been using 19h (24h format) — both are understood because context makes the meaning clear.

préférer — an accent-shifting verb

Most -er verbs are perfectly regular, but préférer belongs to a small group where the accent changes in the singular and ils/elles forms. The stem vowel shifts from é (closed) to è (open) when the syllable is closed — that is, when it ends in a pronounced consonant.

FormStemWhy
je préfèreè [ɛ]ending is silent → accent opens
nous préféronsé [e]ending is pronounced → accent stays closed

Other verbs follow this same pattern: espérer, répéter, considérer.

je pense que… — "I think that…"

This is a very useful structure for expressing opinions. que links penser to a complete clause. Unlike in English, que cannot be omitted — you must always include it.

Je pense que je préfère 19 heures.

The same structure works with other verbs: je crois que (I believe that), je trouve que (I find that), il dit que (he says that).

Korean speaker note

In Korean, time expressions typically come before the main verb: "7시 30분에 먹어요." In French the structure is similar — time expressions usually appear at the start or end of the sentence. Nous mangeons à 19 heures or À 19 heures, nous mangeons — both are natural.

avoir — to have

Avoir is the second essential verb after être. It is irregular — the forms must be memorised. You will use avoir constantly: to say you have something, to form compound tenses later, and in key fixed expressions.

avoir — présent

Note the liaison: nous avons [nu‿zavɔ̃], vous avez [vu‿zave], ils ont [il‿zɔ̃]. The s of nous/vous and the z of ils/elles all link to the following vowel.

j'aimerais + infinitive — the polite request

J'aimerais (I would like) is the standard polite way to make a request in French. It is strongly preferred over je veux (I want) in formal situations — a restaurant, a shop, a phone call — where je veux can sound blunt.

J'aimerais réserver une table pour deux.

The verb after j'aimerais stays in the infinitive — it does not conjugate. This makes it easy to use with any verb you know.

J'aimerais manger ici. — I'd like to eat here.

votre / notre — your / our (formal)

These possessive adjectives go with vous and nous. Unlike ton/ta (for tu), both votre and notre stay the same before masculine and feminine nouns.

In this lesson, the restaurant employee uses votre when addressing Jacques: Quel est votre nom? Jacques would use votre right back if speaking to the restaurant.

bon used as a filler

As an adjective, bon means "good." But at the start of a sentence, bon functions more like "all right" or "well then" — it signals acceptance or a transition, not that something is literally good.

Bon, d'accord pour 18 h 30. — All right, 6:30 works.

Grammaire

Negation — ne… pas

To make a sentence negative in French, wrap the verb with ne before it and pas after it. The verb stays between the two.

PositiveNegative
J'aime la pizza.Je n'aime pas la pizza.
Il mange de la viande.Il ne mange pas de viande.
Nous aimons le vin.Nous n'aimons pas le vin.

When ne comes before a vowel or silent h, it contracts to n' — exactly like je becomes j'. This is elision.

Je n'aime pas la pizza. — I don't like pizza.

de la / du → de after negation

When a sentence is negative, the partitive articles de la, du, and des all change to plain de (or d' before a vowel). The definite article le/la/les stays unchanged.

PositiveNegative
PartitiveJe mange de la viande.Je ne mange pas de viande.
DefiniteJ'aime la pizza.Je n'aime pas la pizza.

The difference: de la viande = some meat (a quantity); la pizza = pizza in general (a category). Negation affects the quantity word, not the category word.

prêt à + infinitive

Prêt (ready) is followed by à and an infinitive — exactly like "ready to" in English. The adjective agrees with the subject.

MasculineFeminine
singularIl est prêt à commander.Elle est prête à commander.
pluralIls sont prêts.Elles sont prêtes.

Korean speaker note

Korean negation uses different verb endings or adds 안/못 before the verb: 먹지 않아요 / 안 먹어요. French always uses the same frame — ne… pas around the conjugated verb — regardless of the verb or subject. Once you know the pattern, it applies everywhere.

Prononciation — Leçon 6
nə… pa ne… pas — the rhythm of negation

In careful, formal speech, both ne and pas are pronounced. In everyday speech, the ne (or n') is often very reduced or barely audible — it becomes part of the rhythm rather than a clear syllable.

The important thing for comprehension: pas is always there and clearly pronounced. If you hear pas after a verb, it is a negative sentence.

💡 At this stage, always pronounce ne clearly in your own speech. You will hear it reduced in others' speech, especially in Quebec French — but understand that je n'aime pas and j'aime pas mean exactly the same thing.
